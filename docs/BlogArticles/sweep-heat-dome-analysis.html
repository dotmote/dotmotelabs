Making Extreme Heat-Dome Analysis More Scalable with SWEEP

Aji John November 2026

Share:

When the Pacific Northwest experienced an exceptional heat dome in June 2021, researchers wanted to understand not just how hot it became, but how forests responded across an entire growing season—and whether those changes persisted. Answering that question meant looking beyond a single image or site. Researchers assembled a multi-year record of satellite observations across forest types and locations, then compared seasonal canopy greenness before, during, and after the event.

The peer-reviewed study focused on Ellsworth Creek Preserve, a 33 km² landscape in Washington managed by The Nature Conservancy (TNC). Much of the preserve was formerly industrial timberland; today, it is part of a watershed-scale experiment exploring how forest management can restore old-growth characteristics. The study compared clearcut, old-growth remnant, thinned, and unthinned control sites, bringing a broad range of canopy conditions into the same analysis. Read the published study.

Nine years of satellite observations across a TNC preserve

The team used Planet Labs' PlanetScope imagery, a four-band surface-reflectance product collected by the SuperDove satellite fleet at approximately 3–5 m ground resolution. The study assembled imagery spanning nine years, from 2017 through 2025, for 120 plots distributed across the preserve. Sixty plots came from the established vegetation-monitoring network in thinned and control stands; additional plots represented clearcuts and old-growth remnants.

For each plot, the researchers worked with a 30 m by 30 m image area centered on the site and derived spectral summaries—including the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), a measure of vegetation greenness. Images with more than 15% cloud cover were excluded. Applying consistent preparation and extraction steps across years, locations, and forest conditions is a substantial part of turning satellite imagery into a dataset that can answer ecological questions.

How SWEEP supported the imagery workflow

The Planet imagery was downloaded and prepared for analysis through SWEEP, a cloud-agnostic workflow execution provider. SWEEP helped support the computational work needed to carry the imagery-processing workflow across a multi-year collection and a network of sites within a 33 km² preserve. Rather than treating every image and plot as a separate manual task, researchers could organize the processing as a workflow—an important foundation for applying consistent analysis steps across a large environmental dataset.

This kind of orchestration matters when a research question depends on both temporal depth and geographic coverage. A single scene can offer a snapshot; a workflow that processes observations across nine years and many plots makes it possible to compare seasonal patterns, forest conditions, and changes over time. SWEEP's role was to support that data-preparation process, helping connect Planet's observations to the downstream statistical analysis.

The analysis then examined seasonal NDVI patterns, including how greenness changed through the growing season and how those patterns differed between the years before, during, and after the 2021 event. The researchers could evaluate the heat dome in a longer context because the imagery workflow provided comparable observations across multiple years—not just a before-and-after pair.

What the study found

Across the study sites, 2021 stood out: NDVI declined over the growing season, unlike the more stable or greening seasonal trends observed in most comparison years. The analysis found suppression across forest types during the heat-dome year, while statistical comparisons provided limited evidence that canopy density changed the magnitude of that suppression. Seasonal patterns moved back toward their earlier trajectories in subsequent years, but overall greenness—especially peak seasonal greenness—had not fully returned to pre-event levels in the period examined.

These findings illustrate why a long, consistent record matters. It helps distinguish an unusual season from ordinary year-to-year variation and lets researchers examine recovery as a trajectory rather than infer it from a single follow-up observation. The study's results concern remotely sensed canopy greenness; they do not by themselves establish every mechanism behind forest response.

Explore the research

The paper describes the study design, imagery, statistical methods, and findings in detail. For researchers working with satellite data, it is also an example of how workflow orchestration can support repeatable preparation of imagery across years and sites—so that analysis can focus on the environmental questions that matter.
